Geographical Background

India extent: 8°4'–37°6'N · 68°7'–97°25'E · Tropic of Cancer crosses the country

India lies entirely in the northern hemisphere between latitudes 8°4' and 37°6' N and longitudes 68°7' and 97°25' E. It measures about 3,214 km north-south and 2,933 km east-west.

Land borders: Afghanistan & Pakistan (NW), China, Bhutan, Nepal (N), Myanmar (far east), Bangladesh (E). Sri Lanka is separated by the Palk Strait and Gulf of Mannar. Country divided into six zones: north, south, east, west, central, north-east — 28 states, 8 union territories.

Word Bank
Latitude/Longitude
— imaginary lines used to fix a place's position on Earth; latitude runs east-west (measures north-south distance from the equator), longitude runs north-south (measures east-west distance from Greenwich).
Strait
— a narrow strip of sea connecting two larger bodies of water and separating two landmasses (e.g., Palk Strait between India & Sri Lanka).
Union Territory
— a region administered directly by the central government, unlike a state which has its own elected government with more autonomy.

Physical Features

Himalayas → Indo-Gangetic Plains → Peninsular Plateau → Coast
  • Himalayas — three near-parallel ranges over ~2,400 km, width 240–320 km; includes Kashmir & Kullu valleys.
  • Indo-Gangetic Plains — ~2,400 km long, 240–320 km broad; formed by Indus, Ganga & Brahmaputra basins; one of the world's most densely populated & flattest alluvial tracts (only 200 m drop in elevation from Delhi to the Bay of Bengal over ~1,600 km).
  • Thar Desert — two parts: the "great desert" (Rann of Kutch to beyond the Luni river) and the "little desert" (Luni between Jaisalmer & Jodhpur).
  • Deccan Peninsular Plateau — marked off by Aravali, Vindhya, Satpura, Maikala, Ajanta ranges (460–1,220 m). Flanked by Eastern Ghats (avg. ~610 m) and Western Ghats (915–1,220 m, peaks over 2,440 m). Nilgiri Hills mark where the two Ghats meet; Cardamom Hills continue the Western Ghats.
Exam angle: East-Bay coastal strip is broader; West-Arabian Sea coastal strip is narrower — a classic PYQ comparison point.
Word Bank
Plateau
— a large, mostly flat area of land raised well above sea level, often with steep sides (the Deccan Plateau is one huge example).
Peninsula
— a piece of land almost entirely surrounded by water but still joined to the mainland (South India forms the Deccan Peninsula, flanked by two seas).
Ghats
— literally "steps"; here it means the mountain ranges running along India's east and west coasts (Eastern Ghats, Western Ghats), not the river steps meaning of the word elsewhere.
Alluvium
— fine, fertile soil made of sand, clay and silt deposited by flowing rivers; it's what makes the Indo-Gangetic plains so good for farming.

Geological Structure

Tap a region chip or a label to know how the three regions differ.

The peninsular region is an inverted triangle, a storehouse of economic minerals, relatively geologically stable, with rock dating back 3.8 Ga (~380 crore years). Rest covered by the Gondwana Supergroup, Deccan Trap lava flows, and younger quaternary sediments.

Indian subcontinent = 3 regions: Extra-Peninsular (Himalayas), Indo-Gangetic Plains, Peninsular region. Current configuration from collision of the Indian Plate with the Eurasian Plate ~4–5 crore years ago (subduction formed the Himalayas). Before collision, the area was submerged under the Tethys Ocean.

Word Bank
Ga (Giga annum)
— a unit for extremely old geological time = 1 billion years (~100 crore years); "3.8 Ga" means 3.8 billion years old.
Gondwana Supergroup
— a very old group of rock layers (sedimentary formations) found across peninsular India, named after the ancient supercontinent Gondwana that India was once part of.
Deccan Trap
— a huge stack of solidified lava layers covering much of central-western India, formed by massive volcanic eruptions millions of years ago.
Quaternary sediments
— the youngest, most recently deposited layers of soil/rock, formed within roughly the last 26 lakh years.
Subduction
— when one of Earth's tectonic plates is forced underneath another as they collide; here, this process pushed up the Himalayas.
Tethys Ocean
— an ancient sea that once existed between the Indian and Eurasian landmasses before they collided; its seabed sediments now form part of the Himalayas.

Four groups: (i) Himalayan rivers (snow/glacier-fed, perennial); (ii) Deccan rivers (rain-fed, fluctuating, mostly non-perennial); (iii) Coastal rivers (short, small catchments, mostly non-perennial); (iv) Inland drainage basin rivers (western Rajasthan — few & scattered).

Word Bank
Perennial river
— a river that flows all year round, fed by snowmelt/glaciers or heavy consistent rainfall (e.g., Ganga, Indus).
Non-perennial river
— a river that dries up or has very low flow for part of the year, usually because it depends only on seasonal rain.
Tributary
— a smaller river or stream that flows into a larger main river (e.g., the Yamuna is a tributary of the Ganga).
Drainage basin
— the entire area of land whose rainwater/runoff eventually drains into one river system.
Catchment area
— the land area that "catches" rainfall and channels it into a particular river or reservoir.

Cyclones: North Indian Ocean (Bay of Bengal + Arabian Sea) shows a bimodal pattern — primary peak post-monsoon, secondary peak pre-monsoon (unlike other world ocean basins with single peak).

Word Bank
Convective weather
— weather caused by warm air rising rapidly, which cools and forms clouds/storms — the reason for sudden summer thunderstorms.
Onset / Withdrawal (of monsoon)
— "onset" is when the monsoon rains first arrive in a region; "withdrawal" is when they retreat and stop.
Synoptic scale system
— a large weather system spanning hundreds to thousands of kilometres, like a cyclone, studied using weather maps.
Bimodal
— having two peaks instead of one; here it means cyclone frequency rises twice a year (before and after monsoon) rather than just once.

Botanical Survey of India count: 22,368 angiosperms, 83 gymnosperms, 1,325 pteridophytes, 2,850 bryophytes, 3,151 lichens, 15,986 fungi, 9,117 algae, 1,297 microbes.

11 Phytogeographical Zones: North-West Himalayas · Indo-Gangetic Plains · Eastern Himalayas · Assam · Central India · Arid Zone · Northern Western Ghats & Coast · Southern Western Ghats, Coast & Lakshadweep · Deccan · Eastern Ghats & Coromandel Coast · Andaman & Nicobar Islands.

  • NW Himalayas: J&K, Ladakh to Kumaon via Himachal — Sal (tropical/sub-tropical), Pine/Boreal (temperate), Deodar/Blue pine/Silver-fir/Quercus (alpine), meadows up to 4,750 m.
  • Indo-Gangetic Plains: Punjab, Haryana, UP, Bihar, parts of Jharkhand/WB — Sal, mixed & swamp forests; mangroves (Rhizophora, Sonneratia, Avicennia).
  • Eastern Himalayas: Sikkim eastward, Darjeeling, Duars, Arunachal — Oaks, Laurels, Maples, Rhododendrons; lower tree-line, less snow than west.
  • Assam: Brahmaputra & Surma valleys — evergreen forests, bamboo, orchids/epiphytes.
  • Central India: MP, Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand — tropical deciduous/thorn forest (Acacia, Albizia, Anogeissus).
  • Arid Zone: W. Rajasthan, Kutch, SW Punjab/Haryana — thorny desert vegetation.
  • Northern Western Ghats & Coast: Goa–Tapti, Konkan/Gujarat — laterite plateau, mangroves, halophytes.
  • Southern Western Ghats: Karnataka, Kerala, TN — montane/shola forests, high endemism.
  • Deccan: rain-shadow, deciduous.
  • Eastern Ghats & Coromandel Coast: Mahanadi delta (Odisha) to Kanyakumari — deciduous, semi-evergreen, evergreen mixed with dry savannas, scrub, estuarine vegetation.
  • Andaman & Nicobar Islands: evergreen, mangrove, beach, alluvial forests; Andaman shows Myanmar floristic affinity, Nicobar closer to Malesian elements; high endemism.
Conservation note: 1,500+ plant species declared "Threatened Species" under Schedule 38 of the Biological Diversity Act, 2002 by respective states.
Word Bank
Phytogeographical zone
— a region defined by having its own distinctive mix of plant life ("phyto" = plant, "geography" = place); India is divided into 11 such zones.
Angiosperms
— flowering plants that produce seeds enclosed inside a fruit (most crops, garden plants, and trees around us).
Gymnosperms
— seed-producing plants without flowers or fruit, with "naked" seeds (e.g., pines, firs, conifers).
Pteridophytes
— non-flowering plants that reproduce via spores instead of seeds (ferns are the classic example).
Bryophytes
— small, simple non-flowering plants without true roots or a vascular (internal water-transport) system, e.g., mosses and liverworts.
Lichens
— a combination organism formed by a fungus and an alga living together; often seen as crusty patches on rocks or tree bark.
Endemic species
— a species found naturally in only one specific geographic area and nowhere else in the world.
Montane / Shola forest
— "montane" means found in mountains; "shola" is the specific stunted, evergreen forest type found in the sheltered valleys of South India's high hill ranges (Western Ghats), often alternating with grassland.
Halophytic / Estuarine vegetation
— "halophytic" plants tolerate salty soil/water; "estuarine" vegetation grows where a river meets the sea (brackish water zones), like mangroves.

India represents two of the world's biogeographic realms — Palearctic and Indo-Malayan — and three biomes: tropical humid forests, tropical dry deciduous forests, warm deserts/semi-deserts.

Protected areas: 1,022 protected areas covering 1,78,640.69 sq.km (~5.43% of India's geographical area). ZSI adds 500–600 new species discoveries/records every year.

Marine & aquatic biodiversity: Coastline of 7,516.6 km (mainland + Lakshadweep + A&N Islands); India has the 18th largest EEZ in the world (2.37 million sq.km). Species recorded: 20,444 marine, 9,457 freshwater, 3,939 estuarine, 5,747 mangrove-ecosystem species. 7,076 species feature on the IUCN Red List.

Wetlands & Biosphere Reserves: 96 Ramsar sites (8,213 species documented by ZSI). 18 Biosphere Reserves under UNESCO's Man and Biosphere programme — Sunderbans alone hosts 2,626 animal species. Of the world's 35 biodiversity hotspots, India shares 4: Himalayas, Indo-Burma, Sundaland, Western Ghats-Sri Lanka.

Ongoing ZSI monitoring: Since Jan 2021, tagging of 8,000+ Olive Ridley turtles (Odisha coast) and 900+ leatherback turtles (Great Nicobar) to track Bay of Bengal/Indian Ocean migration. 37 mammal species genetically identified in the Himalayas via non-invasive techniques; population genetics studies on Arunachal macaque (Macaca munzala), barking deer, and Chinese pangolin. Long-term climate-impact monitoring plots established in Dehang Dibang Biosphere Reserve, Arunachal Pradesh (with Forest Survey of India).

Word Bank
Megadiverse country
— one of a small group of nations (17 in total) that together hold the majority of the world's plant & animal species; India is one of them.
Biodiversity hotspot
— a region that is both extremely rich in unique species AND under serious threat from habitat loss — meaning conservation there is urgent.
Biome
— a large natural community of plants & animals shaped by climate, e.g., "tropical dry deciduous forest" or "warm desert."
Bio-geographic realm/zone
— a large-scale division of the Earth (realm) or of India (zone) based on shared evolutionary history and similar wildlife.
EEZ (Exclusive Economic Zone)
— the stretch of sea (up to 200 nautical miles from the coast) where a country has special rights to explore and use marine resources.
Ramsar site
— a wetland officially recognised as internationally important under the Ramsar Convention, an international treaty for wetland conservation.
Biosphere Reserve
— a large protected area (bigger than a typical sanctuary) that balances wildlife conservation with sustainable human use, recognised under UNESCO's Man and Biosphere Programme.
IUCN Red List
— the world's most authoritative inventory ranking species by extinction risk (from "Least Concern" to "Critically Endangered" to "Extinct").
Endemism
— the degree to which species in an area are found nowhere else on Earth; "high endemism" = many unique, one-of-a-kind species.

PYQ favourite: Literacy rate definition — age 7+, can both read & write with understanding (pre-1991: under-5s automatically illiterate). CBR/CDR/IMR are all "per 1,000" measures — don't confuse denominators.
Word Bank
Decadal growth rate
— the percentage increase in population over a 10-year (Census-to-Census) period.
Sex ratio
— number of females per 1,000 males in a population; used to check gender balance/imbalance in a society.
Total Fertility Rate (TFR)
— the average number of children a woman would have in her lifetime at current birth-rate patterns.
Replacement-level fertility
— the TFR (≈2.1) at which a population exactly replaces itself from one generation to the next, without growing or shrinking (ignoring migration).
Crude Birth/Death Rate (CBR/CDR)
— "crude" here means a simple, un-adjusted rate: total births (or deaths) in a year divided by the mid-year population, ×1,000.
Infant Mortality Rate (IMR)
— the number of babies who die before their first birthday, per 1,000 live births in that year — a key indicator of a country's healthcare quality.

Source: Office of the Registrar General & Census Commissioner of India (decadal Census + Sample Registration System).
